## Page 2 · How this pack works (NEW)

> Design note: NEW page. Place the seven words around a circle, echoing the red dot motif, with ‘you’ in the centre.

**One pack, seven things you can do**

Earlier Voice Packs came in stages: first knowing your body, then finding the words, then speaking up, then changing things. Young people taught us that real life doesn’t come in stages. You might need the words and the courage and the right service all in the same afternoon.

So this edition is built as one connected model, the Aletheia loop. Every tool in it is tagged with the parts of the loop it helps with, and you can start wherever you are.

1. **Understand**: Know what’s happening and the words for it.
2. **Articulate**: Turn what you feel into a clear symptom story.
3. **Navigate**: Know where to go, what it costs, what to bring.
4. **Advocate**: Hold your ground when you’re not heard.
5. **Connect**: Find people who get it, and who can help.
6. **Act**: Take the next step: follow-up, feedback, referral.
7. **Influence**: Add your voice to change what others face.

**Start where you are**
- Something feels off → Health 101
- I have an appointment → Your symptom story
- I wasn’t heard → Fighting medical dismissal
- I want to change things → From me to we

*Health education, not medical advice. In an emergency, call 000.*

## Page 3 · Health 101: Vocabulary · Language is Power

> Design note: keep the April intro page design.

There are moments when something feels wrong in your body, but you don’t know how to explain it. You say “I just feel off”, and the conversation moves on without you. Not because your experience isn’t real, but because it hasn’t been named.

This section gives you those names. Not so you can diagnose yourself, but so you can recognise patterns, ask better questions and stand your ground. You don’t need to become an expert in medicine. You only need enough language to be heard.

## Page 4 · Period pain

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

*medical word: dysmenorrhoea*

**What it is.** Cramping pain in the lower tummy, back or thighs around a period, caused by the uterus contracting. Common, especially in the teens.

**What it can feel like.** Aching or cramping waves, sometimes with nausea, diarrhoea or headaches.

**Worth checking when.** It stops you going to school or doing things, the usual pain relief doesn’t help, it’s getting worse each year, or you have pain outside your period.

**You can say:**
- “My period pain stops me going to school, and the usual pain relief doesn’t help.”
- “It’s been getting worse every year.”

*Loop: Understand · Articulate*

## Page 5 · Heavy menstrual bleeding

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** Bleeding heavy enough to affect your physical, social or emotional life. It’s common, and it can be treated.

**What it can feel like.** Changing a pad or tampon every 1–2 hours, bleeding through at night, clots bigger than a 50-cent coin, periods over 7 days, feeling tired or dizzy.

**Worth checking when.** Any of the above. Heavy periods are a common cause of low iron. Urgent: soaking a pad every hour for several hours, or feeling faint.

**You can say:**
- “I’m changing a pad every hour or two on my heavy days.”
- “Could we check my iron levels?”

*Loop: Understand · Articulate*

## Page 6 · Endometriosis

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

*en-doh-mee-tree-OH-sis*

**What it is.** Tissue similar to the lining of the uterus grows outside it, causing inflammation and pain. About 1 in 7 people assigned female at birth in Australia are diagnosed by their late 40s. It can start in the teens.

**What it can feel like.** Severe period pain, pain between periods, pain with bowel movements or weeing, pain during sex, fatigue.

**Why it gets missed.** Symptoms are often normalised as ‘just bad periods’. Australia’s national guideline puts the average delay to diagnosis at 6 to 8 years.

**Worth checking when.** Period pain stops your life, or pain is getting worse over time. A GP can refer you to a gynaecologist. Diagnosis may involve an ultrasound, MRI or keyhole surgery.

**You can say:**
- “My pain affects my daily life. Could this be something like endometriosis?”
- “What would it take to investigate that?”

*Loop: Understand · Articulate · Advocate*

## Page 7 · PMOS (formerly PCOS)

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

*polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome*

**What it is.** A common hormonal and metabolic condition affecting about 8–13% of women of reproductive age. In May 2026 it was renamed from polycystic ovary syndrome, because it affects the whole body and many people with it don’t have cysts. You’ll still hear “PCOS” for a while.

**What it can feel like.** Irregular or missing periods, acne, extra hair on the face or body, hair thinning on the head, changes in weight or blood sugar.

**Good to know.** In teenagers, diagnosis needs both irregular cycles and signs of higher androgen (‘male’) hormones. An ultrasound isn’t used to diagnose PCOS until 8 years after periods start.

**You can say:**
- “My cycle has been irregular for a long time, and I’ve had acne and extra hair growth too.”
- “Could this be PMOS (PCOS)? What tests would help?”

*Loop: Understand · Articulate*

## Page 8 · Hormonal changes

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** Hormones regulate your cycle, mood, energy, skin and sleep. They shift naturally through puberty and across each cycle.

**Worth checking when.** Ongoing symptoms that disrupt your life: very irregular cycles, severe mood changes before periods, persistent acne, unexplained tiredness.

**Good to know.** ‘Hormonal imbalance’ is a phrase you’ll hear a lot, but it isn’t a diagnosis. If someone says it, it’s fair to ask which hormone, and what test would show it.

**You can say:**
- “Which hormone do you think is involved?”
- “Is there a test that would show that?”

*Loop: Understand · Articulate*

## Page 9 · Iron deficiency

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** Not having enough iron. It’s common in young people with periods, especially heavy ones, and can lead to anaemia (low red blood cells).

**What it can feel like.** Tiredness that doesn’t lift, being short of breath, dizziness, pale skin, poor concentration, feeling cold.

**Worth checking when.** You’re always exhausted, especially if your periods are heavy. It’s checked with a simple blood test.

**You can say:**
- “I’m exhausted all the time and my periods are heavy. Could we check my iron?”

*Loop: Understand · Articulate*

## Page 10 · Anxiety

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** Your body’s alarm system. Useful in small doses. It becomes a problem when it’s frequent, intense, or out of proportion to what’s happening.

**What it can feel like.** Constant worry, racing thoughts, trouble sleeping, a fast heartbeat, nausea, tight muscles, avoiding things.

**Worth checking when.** It’s most days, for weeks, or it stops you doing things. headspace (12–25) and your GP are good first stops.

**You can say:**
- “I’ve felt anxious most days for a while, not just occasionally.”
- “It’s affecting school and sleep. What support is there?”

*Loop: Understand · Articulate · Connect*

## Page 11 · Depression

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** More than sadness. A health condition affecting how you think, feel and function over weeks or longer. It isn’t a personal failure.

**What it can feel like.** Low, flat or numb, losing interest in things, changes in sleep or appetite, low energy, feeling worthless or hopeless.

**Worth checking when.** It’s lasted two weeks or more. If you have thoughts of suicide or self-harm, get help now: call Lifeline on 13 11 14, Kids Helpline on 1800 55 1800, or 000 in an emergency.

**You can say:**
- “I haven’t felt like myself for a while.”
- “I think I need support with my mental health.”

*Loop: Understand · Articulate · Connect*

## Page 12 · ADHD

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** A neurodevelopmental difference in how the brain regulates attention, activity and impulses. In girls it’s often the less visible, inattentive type.

**What it can feel like.** Losing focus, forgetting things, starting lots and finishing little, time slipping, restlessness inside, exhaustion from masking.

**Why it gets missed.** It can be mistaken for being disorganised, dreamy, anxious or ‘not trying’. Many girls are diagnosed later than boys.

**Worth checking when.** It’s been going on since childhood and affects school, work or relationships. A GP can refer you for assessment.

**You can say:**
- “I’ve struggled with focus and organisation for as long as I can remember.”
- “Could I be assessed for ADHD?”

*Loop: Understand · Articulate · Advocate*

## Page 13 · Sensory overload

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** When your brain gets more input (noise, light, touch, crowds) than it can process at once. Common for autistic and ADHD people, and for anyone who’s unwell, exhausted or stressed.

**What it can feel like.** Suddenly overwhelmed, irritable or panicky. Needing to leave, cover your ears or go quiet.

**At appointments.** You can ask for adjustments at appointments: a quieter room, dimmer lights, fewer people, written information.

**You can say:**
- “I’m feeling overwhelmed by the room. Could we dim the lights or find somewhere quieter?”

*Loop: Articulate · Advocate*

## Page 14 · Fatigue

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** Persistent exhaustion that doesn’t go away with rest. Different from normal tiredness after a busy day.

**What it can feel like.** Waking up drained, brain fog, simple tasks taking huge effort.

**Worth checking when.** It’s lasted weeks, it’s affecting your life, or it comes with other symptoms. It has many possible causes (iron, thyroid, mental health, sleep, infections), so it deserves a proper look.

**You can say:**
- “This isn’t normal tiredness. I’m exhausted even after resting.”
- “Can we look for possible causes?”

## Page 15 · Chronic pain

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** Pain lasting more than three months. Over time the nervous system can become more sensitive, so pain can continue even when tissues have healed. It is real pain.

**What it can feel like.** Constant or recurring pain that affects sleep, mood, concentration and movement.

**Why it gets missed.** When nothing shows up on a scan, people are sometimes told it’s ‘in their head’. Pain doesn’t have to be visible to be valid.

**You can say:**
- “This pain has been ongoing for months and it affects my daily life.”
- “Can we talk about a plan to manage it, not just the cause?”

## Page 17 · Inflammation

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** The immune system’s response to injury or infection. Short-term, it helps you heal. Long-term inflammation is part of some conditions, like endometriosis, inflammatory bowel disease and arthritis.

**Good to know.** ‘Inflammation’ on its own is a process, not a diagnosis. Some blood tests show it, but not all inflammation shows up in them. The useful question is what might be causing it.

**You can say:**
- “Could there be an inflammatory cause for this?”
- “What tests would help show that?”

**What it is.** Working out what’s causing your symptoms, based on your story, an examination and sometimes tests. It can take several appointments.

**Good to know.** A doctor may give a ‘working diagnosis’ while they rule other things out. It’s fair to ask what else is being considered.

## Page 19 · Informed consent

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** Agreeing to a test or treatment after understanding what it is, why it’s suggested, the risks and the alternatives. You can ask questions, take time, say no or change your mind.

**If you’re under 18.** Under 18, you can usually consent yourself if the doctor thinks you understand the decision (a ‘mature minor’). The exact rules vary by state.

**You can say:**
- “Can you explain what this involves?”
- “What are my options, including waiting?”
- “I’d like some time to think about it.”

## Page 20 · Confidentiality

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** Health professionals must keep your information private, except in limited cases, such as a serious risk of harm.

**If you’re young.** From 14, Medicare generally needs your consent before releasing your claims information to parents. From 15 you can have your own Medicare card.

**You can say:**

## Page 21 · Implicit bias

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** Assumptions people hold without realising, which shape whose pain is believed and what gets investigated. In Victoria’s Inquiry into Women’s Pain, one in three women said they’d felt dismissed or unheard by practitioners.

**Why it matters.** Bias isn’t always intentional, but it has real effects. Recognising it can help you see that being dismissed isn’t about you.

**You can say:**
- “I don’t feel my concerns have been fully understood yet.”
- “Can we take another look at my symptoms?”

## Page 22 · Referral

> Design note: keep the April vocabulary page layout (echo-type heading, body text, ‘You can say’ bubbles). Add the loop chips as small tags at the bottom.

**What it is.** A letter from your GP to a specialist or service. For most specialists you need one to get a Medicare rebate.

**Ask about.** Ask why, who, how much it costs, how long the wait is, and how long the referral lasts. Public clinics may cost less but have longer waits.

**You can say:**
- “Would a specialist help here?”
- “Is there a public option, or someone closer?”

## Page 25 · Your symptom story (NEW)

> Design note: NEW page. Build the six lines as a card with the labels in small caps boxes.

**Your symptom story**

Doctors think in patterns: what, where, when, how much. Turn what you’re feeling into six short lines and you can say it in thirty seconds, even when you’re nervous.

| | Example |
|---|---|
| WHAT | Sharp, cramping pain |
| WHERE | Low tummy, left side, into my back |
| WHEN | First 2 days of every period, for 18 months |
| HOW MUCH | Usually 6, worst 9. Missed 5 school days this term |
| SO FAR | Ibuprofen helps a bit. Heat pack. Not enough |
| I WANT | To find out why, and a plan that works |

Score pain by what it stops you doing: 0 no pain · 2 notice it, carry on · 4 distracting, need a break · 6 hard to focus or move · 8 have to stop and lie down · 10 worst I can imagine. Give your usual number AND your worst.

Track bleeding, pain, mood, energy and missed days for two or three cycles.

## Page 27 · Fighting Medical Dismissal · When you are not heard

> Design note: keep the April intro page.

You might walk out of an appointment knowing something wasn’t right. Maybe you were interrupted, rushed, or told it was “just stress” before anything was explored. That feeling matters.

Medical dismissal isn’t always obvious. It can sound polite. It can come from people who mean well. This section isn’t about confrontation. It’s about clarity: for each moment, what might be going on, what you can say, and the next move. Say it quietly or firmly, however feels natural. Advocacy doesn’t look one way.

## Page 45 · Closing: Your voice in the room

> Design note: unchanged design.

There is no single way to advocate for yourself. Some days, it may look like asking one question. Other days, it may look like holding your ground in a difficult conversation. Both matter.

You do not need perfect words. You do not need complete confidence. You only need the willingness to say:

“This matters to me.”

Being taken seriously isn’t something you earn. It’s something you’re entitled to.

## Page 47 · Before, during and after (revised)

> Design note: replaces ‘How to see a GP’ bullet page; keep its design.

**Before**: write your symptom story; decide your top 1–3 concerns; book a long appointment if needed; choose a support person.

**During**: lead with your main concern; use your symptom story; ask “What could this be?”; repeat back what you heard.

**Wrapping up**: What’s the plan? What should I watch for? When do I come back? Can this be noted in my file?

**After**: write down what was said; keep tracking; book the follow-up; not better? take the next step.

**Will it cost anything?** Bulk-billed GPs charge Medicare directly, so you pay nothing. Private billing means paying up front and claiming part back. Ask when you book: “Do you bulk bill young people?”

## Page 50 · Your rights in Australia

> Design note: merges the April Consent and Confidentiality pages; keep their design.

The Australian Charter of Healthcare Rights applies to everyone, whatever their age:

- **Access**: Healthcare that meets your needs, including interpreters and adjustments.
- **Safety**: Care that’s safe, in a place where you feel safe.
- **Respect**: Being treated with dignity, with your culture, identity, beliefs and choices respected.
- **Partnership**: Asking questions and being involved in decisions, with support people if you want them.
- **Information**: Clear information about your condition, options, risks and costs, in a way you understand.
- **Privacy**: Your personal and health information kept private and confidential.
- **Give feedback**: Commenting on or complaining about your care, and having it taken seriously.

**Consent.** You can ask what a test or treatment involves, why, the risks and alternatives. You can take time, say no, or change your mind. Under 18, you can usually consent yourself if you understand the decision; the rules vary by state.

**Confidentiality.** What you say is private, with limited exceptions such as serious risk of harm. From 14, Medicare generally needs your consent to share claims with parents; from 15 you can have your own card. Sexual health clinics are confidential and often free.

**If something went wrong:** raise it with the clinician or practice → put it in writing → contact your state’s free health complaints body (in Victoria, the Health Complaints Commissioner).

## Page 53 · Care that fits who you are (NEW)

> Design note: NEW page, or split across two.

**Culturally and linguistically diverse.** Ask for a professional interpreter, free at GP appointments. It’s okay to see a doctor without family present. If health is taboo at home, confidential services exist for exactly this. You can say: “Can we speak without my family translating?”

**LGBTQIA+.** You choose what to share. You can correct your name and pronouns, and ask if a service is inclusive. QLife (1800 184 527) offers peer support. You can say: “I use ___ pronouns.”

**Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander.** Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations offer culturally safe care, run by community. 13YARN (13 92 76) is 24/7 crisis support.

**Neurodivergent or disabled.** Ask for adjustments: more time, a quiet room, written information, step-by-step explanations, a support person. You can say: “Could you write that down for me?”

**Young.** Your age is a reason to be thorough, not to stop looking. You can see a GP on your own, and ask to speak alone for part of an appointment. You can say: “Could I have some time with the doctor on my own?”

**Faith and family.** You can ask for care that respects your beliefs, like a doctor of a particular gender, or modesty during an examination. You can say: “Is it possible to see a female doctor?”

## Page 55 · What Aletheia is (and why we exist)

> Design note: unchanged design.

The Aletheia Project is a youth-led not-for-profit. It exists because of a gap many young people feel but can’t always name: the gap between having access to healthcare and actually knowing how to use it.

Aletheia is Ancient Greek for truth. We’re not here to replace healthcare. We’re here to strengthen your position in it, and to make sure young people’s truth reaches the places where decisions get made.

Aletheia focuses on: **health literacy** (understanding your body and the language for it), **self-advocacy** (being able to speak, ask and hold your ground), and **systems change** (young people shaping the services and policies that affect them).
